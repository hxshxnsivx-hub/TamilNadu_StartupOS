# HARD PIGMENT

*A design philosophy for interfaces that refuse to apologise.*

---

## I. The Movement

**Hard Pigment** is the belief that a surface should behave like a wall, not like water. It rejects the vocabulary of the last decade — the drifting card, the easing curve, the translucent panel that slides politely into view — and replaces it with the older, harder logic of applied colour: pigment laid onto ground, edge meeting edge, nothing dissolving into anything else. Where contemporary interface design asks the eye to *follow*, Hard Pigment asks the eye to *arrive*. Composition does the work that motion was hired to do. This is not minimalism; it is density under absolute discipline, and it must read as the product of countless hours of refinement by a practitioner at the very top of their field.

## II. Space and Form

Form is load-bearing. Every rectangle is a slab with real mass, seated on a visible structural rule — never floating, never shadowed into false depth. The governing device is the **lattice**: an unforgiving grid whose lines are permitted to show themselves, dividing the field into registers the way a wall is divided before it is painted. Panels butt directly against one another with hairline separation, or are separated by generous voids of flat ground; there is no soft middle distance. Corners are sharp or barely radiused — a 2px concession at most — because a rounded corner is a hedge, and this work does not hedge. Negative space is not emptiness but *unpainted ground*, as deliberate as any mark, and its proportions must be tuned with painstaking attention until the composition would collapse if a single void were narrowed.

## III. Colour and Material

Colour is mineral, not digital. The palette is drawn from earths and oxides — a deep carbon ground, a fired red, an ochre that carries iron in it, a chalk that reads as lime rather than paper-white — restricted to five or six values and held there without exception. Gradients are forbidden as decoration; where a transition occurs it is a hard boundary, one pigment stopping and another beginning. Surfaces are matte. Nothing glosses, nothing glows, nothing blurs behind frosted glass. The result should feel *applied* — as though colour was mixed, tested, and committed to. Contrast is the primary instrument: the fired red against carbon must strike hard enough to be felt physically, and the calibration between those two values is the single most laboured decision in the work.

## IV. Scale and Rhythm

Typographic scale is violent by design. A display line sits at enormous size with tracking pulled tight until the letters lock into a single mass; beside it, a label whispers at ten pixels, uppercase, tracked wide enough to read as texture rather than language. Nothing occupies the comfortable middle. This extreme ratio — the monumental beside the microscopic — is what produces tension, and it is what separates master-level execution from competent layout. Rhythm comes from **repetition under constraint**: the same rule weight, the same label treatment, the same slab proportion recurring across every register, so that the eye learns the system in seconds and thereafter reads meaning from deviation alone. Numerals are set as data — tabular, oversized, treated with the reverence of instrument readings.

## V. Composition and Hierarchy

Hierarchy is established by **weight and position, never by ornament**. The most important element is the largest and the most saturated; the second is smaller and set on unpainted ground; the third is a tracked label. There is no fourth. Rules — thin lines of pigment — carry structural meaning: a rule beneath a heading is a foundation, a rule beside a panel is a spine, an accent bar at a panel's edge is a classification mark. Asymmetry is preferred to centring, because a composition that balances unequal masses demonstrates control in a way a centred one never can. Every alignment must survive scrutiny at 400% zoom; every optical adjustment must have been made by hand and by eye, not by formula.

## VI. Behaviour

Motion is reduced to near-zero and treated as a defect to be justified rather than a feature to be added. State changes are **instantaneous** — a pigment either covers a surface or it does not. Where feedback is required, it arrives as a shift in colour or an inversion of ground, delivered in under 80 milliseconds, with no easing curve, no travel, no fade. Nothing slides. Nothing reveals itself progressively. The interface is not a performance that unfolds; it is an object that is simply, entirely, already there — and the restraint required to withhold motion is itself the mark of expertise.

## VII. Text

Words are scarce and structural. A display phrase, a set of tracked labels, a numeral. Explanation lives in the arrangement of the parts, not in paragraphs appended to them. When a sentence is unavoidable it is set small, measured to a narrow column, and placed where it supports the composition rather than interrupting it. Every word retained must have earned its position against the alternative of deleting it.

---

> **The standard:** the finished surface must look as though it were laboured over for weeks — every rule weight tested, every void measured, every pigment boundary decided and re-decided — and must be defensible, line by line, as the work of someone at the absolute top of their field.
